faqs:
  - q: What's the difference between a yellowjacket and a paper wasp?
    a: Body, nest, and temperament. Yellowjackets are compact and bright yellow-and-black, nest in enclosed spots — ground burrows and wall voids — and defend those nests aggressively. Paper wasps are slimmer with dangling legs in flight, build open umbrella combs under eaves, and are comparatively mild-mannered. The distinction matters practically — an enclosed yellowjacket nest is the one that produces mass-sting incidents.
  - q: Why do wasps swarm my food in August but not June?
    a: Colony economics. Early in the season, workers hunt live insects to feed larvae. By late summer the colony is at maximum size, larval demand shifts, and workers turn to scavenging sugars and protein — your soda, fruit, burgers, and trash. Utah's late-summer "wasp problem" is mostly this scavenging phase of the western yellowjacket's annual cycle, and it ends abruptly with the first hard frost.
  - q: Are yellowjacket stings dangerous?
    a: A yellowjacket can sting repeatedly, and stings near an enclosed nest often come many at once, which raises the stakes beyond a single paper wasp sting. For most people the result is pain and swelling; for those with venom allergies, stings can be a genuine emergency needing immediate medical care. Multiple stings, stings in the mouth or throat, or any systemic symptoms warrant medical attention regardless of allergy history.
  - q: I mowed over a nest and got stung several times. What should I do?
    a: Get away from the area first and keep going — yellowjackets pursue, and the alarm response can last minutes. Once clear, wash the stings, use a cold pack, and take an antihistamine if you tolerate one. Watch for symptoms away from the sting sites — hives spreading elsewhere on the body, swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing, dizziness, or vomiting all mean call 911 rather than wait. Many stings at once can be a problem even without an allergy, so err toward being seen if the count is high. Then flag the nest location and stay out of that part of the yard until it's dealt with — the colony will be defensive for the rest of the day.
  - q: Do those hanging yellowjacket traps work?
    a: They help suppress scavenging workers around a patio — western yellowjackets respond well to heptyl butyrate lures — but they will not eliminate a nest. Traps are a perimeter tool — place them away from the area you're protecting, not beside the picnic table. If there's an active nest on the property, the nest is the problem, and the trap is not the solution to it.

## Utah's Most Consequential Stinger

Among Utah's many yellow-and-black insects, the western yellowjacket (*Vespula pensylvanica*) is the one that sends people to urgent care. It combines an enclosed, hidden nest, a large late-season colony, energetic nest defense, and a taste for human food — a package no other local stinging insect matches. Understanding its annual cycle is most of understanding the risk.

## One Queen to Thousands

Every colony starts in spring as a single overwintered queen digging into an abandoned rodent burrow or slipping into a wall void. Through summer the colony compounds — by August a successful nest can hold thousands of workers. Then, with cold weather, it collapses entirely; only new queens survive, overwintering alone. This is why nests never persist year to year in the same hole, and why the late-summer peak arrives on schedule every year.

## Ground Nests and Wall Voids

The nests you can't see cause the trouble. Mowing over an unnoticed ground nest is the classic mass-sting scenario in Utah yards; the vibration triggers a colony-wide defensive response. Wall-void nests bring a different hazard — treating the entrance hole with store-bought spray can drive the colony deeper into the wall and occasionally into the house. Both situations are exactly what professional removal is for. Open comb hanging visibly under an eave, by contrast, belongs to the much milder [paper wasp](/pest-library/paper-wasp).

## Reducing the Late-Summer Grind

- Keep trash cans lidded tightly and rinse recycling — scavengers recruit nestmates to reliable food.
- Don't leave pet food, fallen fruit, or hummingbird-feeder drips available.
- Serve outdoor meals with covers handy, and pour sodas into cups — a wasp inside a can is a sting waiting to happen.
- Walk the yard early in the season; a June nest entrance with light traffic is far easier to deal with than the same nest in September.
