## The Sting Behind the Headlines

The Arizona bark scorpion (*Centruroides sculpturatus*) deserves its reputation on medical grounds: it is the one scorpion in the United States whose sting is a genuine medical event, especially for young children. In its core range in Arizona it is a fixture of home life. Utah sits at the very northern edge of that range, and the documented picture here is much narrower.

## What the Evidence Shows

USU Extension's Utah scorpion fact sheet recognizes nine species in the state and gives this one a single Utah location: **Kane County**. That is the honest boundary of current authoritative documentation. It is not proof the species can never turn up elsewhere — ranges shift, and commerce moves animals — but it does mean the common claim that bark scorpions are an established fact of St. George life is not supported by the university research we can cite. When a St. George homeowner brings us "a bark scorpion," it is usually one of the species below.

One naming wrinkle, since it affects anyone checking our source: USU's fact sheet calls Utah's medically significant scorpion *Centruroides exilicauda*. Current usage generally treats the Arizona bark scorpion as *C. sculpturatus* and reserves *C. exilicauda* for a Baja California species; the two were treated as one for decades. Same animal in USU's Kane County record, two names in the literature.

## The Scorpions St. George Homeowners Do Find

- **[Giant desert hairy scorpion](/pest-library/desert-hairy-scorpion)** — Utah's largest scorpion and Washington County's most conspicuous one. Big, robust, painful sting, mild venom.
- **[Northern scorpion](/pest-library/northern-scorpion)** — smaller and ground-dwelling. USU gives its Utah distribution as "all of Utah," the Uintah Basin included.
- **Other natives** — USU's nine Utah species include a second hairy scorpion (*Hadrurus spadix*) plus *Paruroctonus becki*, *P. utahensis*, *Vaejovis confusus*, *Anuroctonus phaiodactylus* and *Serradigitus wupatkiensis*. Robust pincers and ground habits separate them from *Centruroides*.

## Why Identification Is Worth Five Minutes

The species changes the medical calculus. A household with small children in Utah's far south should treat any slender, wall-climbing scorpion with real caution and get stings evaluated quickly; the same household, looking at a heavy-bodied [desert hairy scorpion](/pest-library/desert-hairy-scorpion), is dealing with a bee-sting-class animal. Five minutes with a photo and the build test above is the difference between those two situations — which is worth more than any assumption made from the name alone.
