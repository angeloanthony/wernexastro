## Utah's Native Mound Builder

Look at the ground around the mound before you look at the ants. Harvester ants (*Pogonomyrmex* species) build one of the most recognizable nests in the West — a low gravel disk, sometimes several feet across, sitting at the center of a circle the ants have stripped bare of vegetation. That clearing isn't decoration — it keeps sight lines open and the nest sun-warmed. Colonies live for years, sending foragers out along trunk trails to collect seeds, which they husk and store in underground granaries.

## Why They Get Called Fire Ants

Big red ants plus a painful sting equals "fire ants" in most people's minds — but USU Extension is direct that imported fire ants are "NOT known to occur in Utah." It is equally direct about the caveat, and we would rather print it than hide it: "Parts of Washington, Iron, and Kane Counties may be suitable for colony establishment, particularly in areas that have accessible water from irrigation or natural sources." So the honest answer is not *fire ants could never be here*. It is *they are not here now, and what you are looking at is almost certainly a native*.

The mix-up matters for two reasons. Panic-driven treatments aimed at "fire ants" tend to be the wrong ones. And harvester ants are native and ecologically useful — a colony that isn't threatening anyone doesn't automatically need to die. When the mound is beside a swing set, that calculation changes, and that's a legitimate treatment call.

One more thing worth knowing before anyone panics on either side of this: USU notes that "since many ants look similar to imported fire ants, microscopic examination by a trained entomologist is needed to confirm the identity of suspected fire ants." If you think you have something genuinely unusual, that is a specimen for the diagnostic lab, not a judgement call from a phone photo — ours included.

## Living With (or Without) Them

- Give mounds a wide berth during yard work; stings cluster around nest disturbance.
- Teach kids to recognize the bare-circle mound — it's the ant equivalent of a "keep out" sign.
- On larger properties, mowing around rather than over active mounds avoids provoking defenders.
- If a mound is in a high-traffic or play area, treat the colony properly once rather than skirmishing with it all summer.

## Not Your Mound?

Small sand piles in sidewalk cracks are [pavement ants](/pest-library/pavement-ant). Big ants coming from wood, or indoors in winter, are [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ant). The bare gravel disk in open sun is the harvester signature.
