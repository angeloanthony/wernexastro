## A New Name on the Southwest Utah Roster

The roof rat (*Rattus rattus*) is the dominant rat of warm-winter cities across the Southwest — Phoenix and Las Vegas know it well — and climate has always been the fence keeping it out of most of Utah. St. George sits on the warm side of that fence. Recent local reporting of roof rats in Washington County fits a pattern seen elsewhere: arrival in irrigated, fruit-planted neighborhoods, then quiet entrenchment. We're deliberately careful with claims here — the documentation is recent and local, not university-confirmed and statewide — but "watch your attic" is now reasonable advice in St. George in a way it wasn't a decade ago.

## An Aerial Rodent

Everything distinctive about roof rats follows from their climbing. They travel fence tops, utility lines, and branch highways; enter structures at the roofline rather than the foundation; nest in attics, soffits, and dense trees rather than burrows; and feed heavily on fruit and nuts. An inspection that never leaves ground level can miss a roof rat problem entirely — the action is at the gutter line and above.

## Why Fruit Trees Matter So Much

A backyard citrus, fig, pomegranate, or nut tree in a warm St. George neighborhood is a roof rat commissary. Hollowed fruit still hanging on the tree is near-diagnostic sign. Harvesting promptly, picking up drops, and pruning canopy back from rooflines converts a property from prime habitat to marginal — often the difference between hosting a population and watching one pass through.

## If Something's Running in Your Attic

- Listen for weight and timing: rats are audible, mice are faint, and both are nocturnal.
- Check attic sign safely — droppings scale is the quickest field test — or have it inspected.
- Trim vegetation off the roof and skirt dense cover; deny the aerial approach.
- Seal roofline gaps, vent screens, and utility penetrations at rat scale (they can use surprisingly small openings).
- Get identification confirmed before choosing a control strategy — [house mouse](/pest-library/house-mouse), [deer mouse](/pest-library/deer-mouse), woodrat, and roof rat each call for a different play.
