  - q: How is a roof rat different from a mouse in the attic?
    a: Scale and style. A roof rat is a full-sized rat — body plus tail can exceed a foot — with droppings several times mouse size and footsteps you can hear from the room below. Mice are common in attics too, so sign size is the differentiator worth checking; raisin-scale droppings and audible running favor a rat. The distinction changes the equipment and the entry-point search — mouse-scale sealing won't stop a rat, and rat-scale sign shouldn't be treated with mouse traps.
  - q: What attracts roof rats to a property?
    a: Food overhead and cover overhead. Unharvested fruit and nut trees are the classic driver — roof rats will hollow out citrus on the branch — along with outdoor pet food, birdseed, and open compost. For cover they want dense vegetation touching structures — untrimmed trees against rooflines, thick ivy, and unskirted palms. St. George landscapes can offer all of it, which is why sanitation and trimming are half of any serious response.

## A New Name on the Southwest Utah Roster

For most of Utah, winter has always done the work. The roof rat (*Rattus rattus*) dominates warm-winter cities across the Southwest — Phoenix and Las Vegas know it well — and cold has been the fence keeping it out. St. George sits on the warm side of that fence, which is the whole reason this page exists and a Vernal version of it does not. Recent local reporting of roof rats in Washington County fits a pattern seen elsewhere: arrival in irrigated, fruit-planted neighborhoods, then quiet entrenchment. We're deliberately careful with claims here — the documentation is recent and local, not university-confirmed and statewide — but "watch your attic" is now reasonable advice in St. George in a way it wasn't a decade ago.

## An Aerial Rodent

Everything distinctive about roof rats follows from their climbing. They travel fence tops, utility lines, and branch highways; enter structures at the roofline rather than the foundation; nest in attics, soffits, and dense trees rather than burrows; and feed heavily on fruit and nuts. An inspection that never leaves ground level can miss a roof rat problem entirely — the action is at the gutter line and above.

## Why Fruit Trees Matter So Much

A backyard citrus, fig, pomegranate, or nut tree in a warm St. George neighborhood is a roof rat commissary. Hollowed fruit still hanging on the tree is near-diagnostic sign. Harvesting promptly, picking up drops, and pruning canopy back from rooflines converts a property from prime habitat to marginal — often the difference between hosting a population and watching one pass through.

## Something's Running in Your Attic — Now What

Start with the question you can answer from the room below: how heavy does it sound? Mice are faint enough that people describe them as scratching; a rat is audible as footfall, and once you have heard the difference you do not un-hear it. Both are nocturnal, so the timing tells you nothing.

Then confirm before you buy anything. Droppings scale is the fastest field test — rice grain means mouse, raisin means rat — and the answer genuinely changes the job, because mouse-scale sealing will not stop a rat and rat traps will not catch mice efficiently. Southern Utah adds two more candidates worth ruling out: a native woodrat, which is stockier with a furred tail and builds stick middens rather than nesting in insulation, and a Norway rat, which burrows at ground level rather than living overhead. [House mouse](/pest-library/house-mouse), [deer mouse](/pest-library/deer-mouse), woodrat, roof rat — four different animals, four different plays.

If it is a roof rat, the response happens above head height, which is what makes this a different job from a mouse call. Trim the branch highways off the roofline and skirt the dense cover; seal roofline gaps, vent screens, and utility penetrations at rat scale; and deal with the fruit, because a tree dropping unharvested citrus is the reason the population is worth staying for. An inspection that never leaves the ground can miss all of it.
