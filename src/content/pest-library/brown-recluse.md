## Why Everyone Thinks They've Seen One

The brown recluse has one of the biggest reputations in American pest lore — and one of the most misapplied. Its actual range covers the central Midwest and South and stops far short of Utah. Utah State University Extension, which runs the state's plant pest diagnostic lab and reviews spider submissions from across Utah, is direct about it: brown recluses do not occur here.

What Utahns find instead are lookalikes:

- **Cellar spiders** — long-legged, with a dark smudge on the carapace that people read as a violin. USU notes they "can resemble brown recluse spiders to the untrained eye." USU notes they have "6 eyes and a 'violin' pattern behind their eyes" — so the violin marking on its own settles nothing. They are harmless.
- **Funnel weavers / grass spiders** — fast, brown, and common indoors in fall. Harmless.
- **Hobo spiders** — long blamed for necrotic bites, but USU Extension notes there is no significant scientific evidence that hobo spider bites cause tissue damage.
- **Wolf spiders** — big and alarming, not medically dangerous.

## The One Real Recluse in Utah — and Why It's Not the One You Found

Utah's single recluse-family species is the **desert recluse** (*Loxosceles deserta*), documented only in Washington County in the state's far southwest. It lives outdoors — native vegetation, packrat middens, rodent burrows — and seldom turns up inside homes. Its venom is genuinely necrotic, so it deserves respect, but it is a rare outdoor desert animal, not a closet spider.

## What to Do Instead of Worrying

If you find a suspicious brown spider, capture it in a jar or take a sharp photo and [get it identified](/bug-identifier) — identification is the entire game with recluse reports. And if a spider population in your garage or basement is the real concern, that's a solvable problem regardless of species.
