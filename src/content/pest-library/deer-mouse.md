## Read This Before You Sweep

If you take one thing from this page, take this: **do not dry-sweep or vacuum accumulated mouse droppings in an enclosed space.** Open it up and let it air out first, wet everything down with disinfectant and let it soak, then wipe up with gloves on and bag the waste. Sweeping is the single action most likely to turn a mouse problem into a health problem, because it puts into the air the exact material you must not breathe.

The reason is specific to this species. The Four Corners region is where hantavirus pulmonary syndrome was first recognized, and deer mice are its primary reservoir — USU describes the deer mouse as a "known carrier of Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome, a rare but potentially fatal lung disease found in mouse feces and urine." Utah's health department describes the route the same way: the virus is "usually spread by inhaling the virus, which is in the droppings, urine and saliva of infected rodents."

Keep both halves of that in proportion. The disease is genuinely rare, Utah has recorded only a small number of cases across decades, and a mouse crossing a kitchen floor is not an emergency. What earns real caution is the narrow scenario Basin properties produce so reliably: a shed or cabin or camper closed up for months, a floor of accumulated droppings, and somebody opening the door with a broom in hand. That situation, and the fifteen minutes of ventilation and wet cleanup that defuses it, is what this page exists for.

## The Basin's Signature Rodent

Ask what pest problem defines rural Uintah Basin properties and the answer is the deer mouse (*Peromyscus maniculatus*). It's native, abundant, and superbly adapted to high desert — USU notes it prefers "rural areas including fields, pastures and vegetative areas around buildings" and moves "indoors when it gets cold outside." Every barn, shop, hay shed, cabin, and parked camper between Vernal and Roosevelt is prime winter habitat. Unlike the house mouse, which lives its whole life alongside people, the deer mouse commutes.

## Where to Look on a Basin Property

Deer mice concentrate where seeds, cover, and quiet meet: grain and feed storage, hay stacks, wood piles, stored vehicles and equipment, camper cushions and wiring runs, cabin drawers and mattresses. Engine compartments deserve special mention — nesting deer mice chew wiring insulation, and a winter of mouse occupancy can do real damage to trucks, tractors, and ATVs parked for the season.

## Making a Structure Boring to Mice

- Seal gaps larger than a pencil around foundations, doors, and utility penetrations; steel wool plus sealant beats foam alone.
- Store feed, grain, and pet food in metal or heavy plastic bins with tight lids.
- Elevate and gap firewood and stored materials so cats and open sight lines work against mice.
- Before winter storage, remove food and nesting materials from campers and vehicles, and consider rodent deterrent practices for engine bays.
- Put snap traps or monitored stations in outbuildings each fall rather than waiting for spring's surprise.
