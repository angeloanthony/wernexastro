## The Basin's Signature Rodent

Ask what pest problem defines rural Uintah Basin properties and the honest answer is the deer mouse (*Peromyscus maniculatus*). It's native, abundant, and superbly adapted to high desert — and every barn, shop, hay shed, cabin, and parked camper between Vernal and Roosevelt is prime winter habitat. Unlike the house mouse, which lives its life alongside people, the deer mouse commutes: outdoors all summer, indoors when the cold arrives.

## Why This Mouse Gets Its Own Safety Rules

The Four Corners region is where hantavirus pulmonary syndrome was first recognized, and deer mice are its primary reservoir. The disease is rare — Utah has recorded only a small number of cases — but it is severe enough that public health agencies publish specific cleanup rules for exactly the scenario Basin properties create: an enclosed outbuilding, months of accumulated droppings, and an owner with a broom. The wet-cleanup method (ventilate, soak with disinfectant, wipe wearing gloves — never dry-sweep) removes most of the real-world risk, and it's the single most important thing on this page.

## Where to Look on a Basin Property

Deer mice concentrate where seeds, cover, and quiet meet: grain and feed storage, hay stacks, wood piles, stored vehicles and equipment, camper cushions and wiring runs, cabin drawers and mattresses. Engine compartments deserve special mention — nesting deer mice chew wiring insulation, and a winter of mouse occupancy can do real damage to trucks, tractors, and ATVs parked for the season.

## Making Structures Boring to Mice

- Seal gaps larger than a pencil around foundations, doors, and utility penetrations; steel wool plus sealant beats foam alone.
- Store feed, grain, and pet food in metal or heavy plastic bins with tight lids.
- Elevate and gap firewood and stored materials so cats and open sight lines work against mice.
- Before winter storage, remove food and nesting materials from campers and vehicles, and consider rodent deterrent practices for engine bays.
- Put snap traps or monitored stations in outbuildings each fall rather than waiting for spring's surprise.
