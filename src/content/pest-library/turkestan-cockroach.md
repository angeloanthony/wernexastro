## A Roach Worth Knowing Before It's a Problem

The Turkestan cockroach (*Blatta lateralis*) has rewritten the outdoor-roach map of the American Southwest over the past few decades. Introduced — likely with military equipment returning from central Asia, then spread widely through the feeder-insect trade — it has become the dominant peridomestic roach in many Arizona, Nevada, and California cities, in many places displacing the slower-breeding Oriental cockroach. It matures faster and lays more eggs, and in a desert city it finds ready-made habitat in every irrigation box and block wall.

## Why We're Cautious About the Utah Claim

St. George sits a short drive from established Turkestan cockroach territory, and Southwest pest-trend lists routinely feature the species. But regional momentum is not local evidence: Utah's university and agriculture institutions have not, to our knowledge, published a record establishing it here. This page exists to make the honest middle statement: know what it looks like, and take a suspected sighting seriously enough to get it identified. A new state record starts with somebody photographing one.

## Turkestan or Something Else?

- **Small, striped, strictly indoors, kitchen at night** → [German cockroach](/pest-library/german-cockroach), a completely different (and more urgent) problem.
- **Large, dark, sluggish, near floor drains and damp basements** → likely an Oriental cockroach.
- **Inch-long, reddish male flying to the porch light on a summer night, or a cream-marked dark female in the water-meter box** → photograph it; that's the Turkestan profile.

## If Outdoor Roaches Are Already Your Reality

Whatever the species, outdoor roaches share the same levers: reduce irrigation overspray and standing water, seal ground-level entry gaps, keep garage doors sealed at the sweep, and address the cavity harborages (meter boxes, wall voids, paver edges) where they breed. Identification simply tells us which levers matter most.
