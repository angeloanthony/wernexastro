faqs:
  - q: I saw one mouse. Do I have "mice"?
    a: Assume more than one. House mice are secretive, mostly nocturnal, and breed quickly — a female can produce a litter of five to six pups roughly every month in a warm house with food. A single daytime sighting often indicates a population large enough that some individuals are being pushed into risky open movement.   - q: Are house mice actually harmful, or just gross?
    a: Three real harms. They contaminate far more food than they eat, with droppings and urine reaching pantries and prep surfaces. They gnaw constantly to wear down their teeth, and gnawed wiring is a documented fire hazard. And they can carry bacteria like salmonella on their bodies and in droppings. None of this reaches the hantavirus-level concern associated with deer mice, but it's more than enough reason not to cohabitate.
  - q: Why don't the mice touch my traps?
    a: Usually placement, not intelligence. Mice travel along walls and edges with their whiskers touching a surface, rarely crossing open floor — a trap in the middle of the room may as well not exist. Set traps perpendicular to walls with the trigger end at the baseboard, in the areas droppings actually appear, and use small smears of high-value bait like peanut butter. If well-placed traps still go untouched for days while droppings accumulate, the activity has shifted — follow the fresh sign.
  - q: Do ultrasonic repellers or peppermint oil work?
    a: Not reliably, no. Controlled testing hasn't supported ultrasonic devices, and scent deterrents fade fast against an animal willing to nest in a wall void for the winter. ## The Roommate You Didn't Invite

The house mouse (*Mus musculus*) has been living alongside humans since agriculture began, and it shows: no other vertebrate pest is as thoroughly adapted to our buildings. It needs almost no water (extracting moisture from food), nests within a few feet of a food source, fits through a pencil-width gap, and reproduces every few weeks indoors. In Utah it's the default rodent of towns and subdivisions statewide — the mouse behind the stove rather than the mouse in the barn.

## Turning Signs Into Trap Placement

Homeowners tend to read signs as a yes-or-no question — are there mice? A technician reads them as a map. Where droppings concentrate is where mice are spending time, and that is where traps belong; a scattering in one cabinet and a heavy accumulation behind the range are two different instructions. Rub marks along a baseboard mark a route travelled often enough to leave oil behind, which is a better trap location than any spot chosen by intuition.

The other thing sign tells you is whether you are winning. Clean the droppings out completely once, at the start, and then watch what reappears and where. Fresh sign in a new location a week later means the population has shifted rather than shrunk — usually because traps went where the mouse was seen instead of where it lives.

## Mouse or Something Bigger?

Size the sign honestly. Mouse droppings are rice-grain scale; anything approaching raisin scale, gnaw marks with visible broad tooth grooves, or noises with real weight to them suggest a rat — in the St. George area, see the [roof rat](/pest-library/roof-rat), a recent arrival worth ruling out. And on rural property or in outbuildings, check the belly if you catch one: a white belly and bicolored tail means [deer mouse](/pest-library/deer-mouse), which changes the cleanup safety rules.

## Denying the Invitation

- Walk the exterior at dusk with a flashlight: light visible through a gap from inside a garage means a mouse-sized door.
- Seal utility penetrations, foundation gaps, and vent screens with steel wool plus sealant; replace worn door sweeps, especially garage doors.
- Store pantry staples, pet food, and birdseed in rigid sealed containers — birdseed bags in garages start a remarkable number of Utah mouse problems.
- Declutter cardboard and fabric storage in garages and basements; mice nest in exactly that.
