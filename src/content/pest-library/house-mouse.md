faqs:
  - q: I saw one mouse. Do I have "mice"?
    a: Assume more than one. House mice are secretive, mostly nocturnal, and breed quickly — a female can produce a litter of five to six pups roughly every month in a warm house with food. A single daytime sighting often indicates a population large enough that some individuals are being pushed into risky open movement. Droppings are the honest census — fresh, dark, soft droppings in multiple rooms mean an established and growing presence.
  - q: Are house mice actually harmful, or just gross?
    a: Three real harms. They contaminate far more food than they eat, with droppings and urine reaching pantries and prep surfaces. They gnaw constantly to wear down their teeth, and gnawed wiring is a documented fire hazard. And they can carry bacteria like salmonella on their bodies and in droppings. None of this reaches the hantavirus-level concern associated with deer mice, but it's more than enough reason not to cohabitate.
  - q: Why don't the mice touch my traps?
    a: Usually placement, not intelligence. Mice travel along walls and edges with their whiskers touching a surface, rarely crossing open floor — a trap in the middle of the room may as well not exist. Set traps perpendicular to walls with the trigger end at the baseboard, in the areas droppings actually appear, and use small smears of high-value bait like peanut butter. If well-placed traps still go untouched for days while droppings accumulate, the activity has shifted — follow the fresh sign.
  - q: Do ultrasonic repellers or peppermint oil work?
    a: Not reliably, no. Controlled testing hasn't supported ultrasonic devices, and scent deterrents fade fast against an animal willing to nest in a wall void for the winter. Money spent there is better spent on a tube of sealant, door sweeps, and decent traps — the unglamorous combination that determines whether mice can live in your house.

## The Roommate You Didn't Invite

Nothing has been living in human buildings longer, or gotten better at it. The house mouse (*Mus musculus*) has been our roommate since agriculture began, and no other vertebrate pest is as thoroughly adapted to the way we build. It needs almost no water (extracting moisture from food), nests within a few feet of a food source, fits through a pencil-width gap, and reproduces every few weeks indoors. In Utah it's the default rodent of towns and subdivisions statewide — the mouse behind the stove rather than the mouse in the barn.

## Turning Signs Into Trap Placement

Homeowners tend to read signs as a yes-or-no question — are there mice? A technician reads them as a map. Where droppings concentrate is where mice are spending time, and that is where traps belong; a scattering in one cabinet and a heavy accumulation behind the range are two different instructions. Rub marks along a baseboard mark a route traveled often enough to leave oil behind, which is a better trap location than any spot chosen by intuition.

The other thing sign tells you is whether you are winning. Clean the droppings out completely once, at the start, and then watch what reappears and where. Fresh sign in a new location a week later means the population has shifted rather than shrunk — usually because traps went where the mouse was seen instead of where it lives.

## Mouse or Something Bigger?

Size the sign before naming the animal. Mouse droppings are rice-grain scale; anything approaching raisin scale, gnaw marks with visible broad tooth grooves, or noises with real weight to them suggest a rat — in the St. George area, see the [roof rat](/pest-library/roof-rat), a recent arrival worth ruling out. And on rural property or in outbuildings, check the belly if you catch one: a white belly and bicolored tail means [deer mouse](/pest-library/deer-mouse), which changes the cleanup safety rules.

## The Walk That Ends the Problem

Trapping is the visible half of mouse control and the less important one. The half that decides whether you are done is a slow walk around the outside of your own house, and it is worth doing once, properly, with a flashlight at dusk.

You are looking for light. Stand inside a dark garage and have someone shine a light along the door line and the foundation from outside; every place the light comes through is a hole a mouse can use, because a house mouse fits through a gap about a quarter inch wide — roughly the width of a pencil. Utility penetrations, vent screens, the corners of garage doors where the seal has worn into a curl: those are the doors, and steel wool packed in behind sealant closes them in a way expanding foam alone does not.

Then take the same walk indoors, looking for what feeds them. Birdseed in a paper sack on a garage shelf starts a remarkable number of Utah mouse problems; so does an open bag of dog food, and so does a wall of cardboard boxes, which is simultaneously food, nesting material, and cover. Rigid sealed containers and less cardboard cost nothing and remove the reason to stay.

Do the walk and the traps become a one-time cleanup. Skip it, and the traps become a subscription.
